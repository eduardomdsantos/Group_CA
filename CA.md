# From MySpace to TikTok: The Evolution of Social Media Platforms - A Business Case Study

## 1. Introduction
### 1.1. Context
Social media has gone through three main phases, and each phase changed what the core product was. In the first phase, profile-based networking, platforms like MySpace (2003) and Facebook (2004) were built around a user's personal page and friend list. News Corporation bought MySpace for $580 million in 2005, only to sell it for $35 million six years later after it lost its audience (CBS Boston, 2011). Facebook then introduced the news feed and the "Like" button and grew further by buying out competitors like Instagram for around $1 billion in 2012 (Meta, 2012). Later, TikTok (2017) shifted away from friend lists entirely by using a recommendation algorithm to show content based on users' interests, a trend that artificial intelligence has pushed even further since 2023. Despite all these changes, the underlying business model has stayed the same: the platforms are free to use and make money through advertising.

### 1.2. Significance
These platforms now influence education, everyday community life, and politics for billions of users. According to DataReportal, there were 5.81 billion social media user identities worldwide as of October 2026 (Kemp, 2026). Because these platforms have become so large, managing and regulating them has turned into a major business and policy issue. For example, Australia's under-16 social media ban has been in effect since December 2025 (Australian Government, n.d.), the European Commission proposed the EU KIDS Act in September 2026 to set the minimum age for opening an account at 15 (EEAS, 2026), and TikTok's US operations were restructured into a majority American-owned joint venture in January 2026 (Shepardson, 2026).

### 1.3. Thesis Statement
The growth of social media from MySpace to TikTok has been driven by business models that profit from capturing user attention and collecting data. While this has created real benefits for society, such as easier communication, wider access to information, and open political debate, it has also led to serious problems like distraction, loneliness, and disinformation. Going forward, long-term success will belong to platforms that balance profit with user well-being by building ethical practices into their design and strategy.

### 1.4. Viewpoint Selection
This case study looks at the evolution of social media from two perspectives. The societal viewpoint takes the perspective of an everyday user and looks at how these platforms affect education, local and online communities, and politics. The business viewpoint looks at revenue, competition, and company value across MySpace, Facebook, Instagram, and TikTok. Combining both viewpoints makes sense because the same business decisions that generate profit, such as targeted ads, data tracking, algorithmic feeds, and buying out rivals, are also what cause the biggest social impacts. Finally, the ethical reflection brings both sides together using a stakeholder approach (Cho et al., 2024).

## 2. Historical Development
Social Media has developed significantly since the early 2000s. It has changed how people communicate, share content and use the internet (boyd and Ellison, 2007).
### 2.1. Timeline
#### 2003-2005: Profile and Personalization Era
MySpace was created in August 2003 by Tom Anderson, Chris DeWolfe, and Aber Whitcomb as a project with eUniverse (known as Intermix Media). It offered profile customisation and a platform for independent brands. (boyd and Ellison, 2007).
The Facebook.com launched at Harvard University in February 2004 and it initiated a cleaner and more structured approach to social networking. (Kirkpatrick, 2010).
In February 2005, three former PayPal employees registered YouTube which transformed the internet from a text and photo medium into a hub for user generated videos. (Burges and Green, 2009).

#### 2006 - 2008: Change of Guard and Microblogging
In March 2006, Twitter entered the scene with a 140 character limit and popularised real time microblogging and worldwide news delivery. (Kwat et al., 2010).
In November 2006, Google bought YouTube for 1.65 billion dollars after recognising the growth potential. (Burges and Green, 2009).
In 2008, Facebook surpassed MySpace in monthly active users and officially claimed market dominance. (Kirkpatrick, 2010).

#### 2010 - 2012
In October 2010, Kevin Systrom and Mike Krieger launched Instagram as an IOS only app, which leveraged the upgraded camera of the iPhone 4 to focus fully on filtered photography and immediate liking mechanics. (Frier, 2020).
In July 2011 , Evan Spiegel and Bobby Murphy launched Snapchat. They redefined social habits among younger people through disappearing photos and messages. (Colao, 2012).
In April 2012, Facebook bought a fast growing Instagram for 1 billion dollars. (Rusli, 2012).

#### 2016 - Present
In 2016 ByteDance launched Douyin in China which was followed up by TikTok in 2017. TikTok disrupted traditional social networks by using a hyper accurate content recommendation algorithm instead of a user's friend graph. (Kaye, Chen and Zeng, 2021).
In 2020-2021, YouTube Shorts and Instagram Reels were introduced to compete with TikTok's vertical video interface. (Instagram, 2020).
Between the years 2022-2024, Elon Musk ended up buying Twitter for 44 billion dollars and rebranded it to X. In between these years Facebook also made history by becoming the first social networking platform to surpass 3 billion monthly active users. (Meta Platforms, 2023)

#### 2023 - Present: Artificial Intelligence Era
Artificial Intelligence (AI) has changed social media through personalised recommendations, chatbots and targeted advertisement. Platforms that we have looked at already like TikTok, Instagram and Facebook use AI to suggest content based on users' interests. (Narayanan, 2023). However, AI has also increased concerns about privacy and fake information. (Bontridder and Poullet, 2021). Nowadays it still continues to shape how people communicate online.

## 3. Societal Viewpoint Analysis
### 3.1. Definition of the Societal Point of View:

A societal viewpoint is a perspective of society on a topic. In case of this case study, it will examine how the growth and evolution of social media affected multiple social areas such as: Education, quality of life in communities and politics.

### 3.2. The impact:
#### 3.2.1. Education:

As social media has bocame more integrated into education it has brought its drawbacks and benefits, the impact of that adaptation is shown below: 

##### Positive: 

- Stay in touch: Students have easier time communicating and socializing  with each other via shared platforms like Instagram, TikTok and Snapchat.
- Information: It became much easier for students to find information by themselves and study independently (OECD, 2026).

##### Negative: 

- Distraction: It has become a common issue that social media platforms distract students from their work, whether it is at home or in class they choose social media and entertainment over a lecture or a class (OECD, 2026).
- Focus: There is a connection between long exposure to the format of short videos that don't require concentration and future problems with focusing on long, mundane tasks. "A significant negative relationship between MPSVATQ and theta power index reflecting the executive control in the prefrontal region" (Frontiers of neuroscience, 2024) .

#### 3.2.2. Communities

The development of social media has led to huge changes in how people communicate and interact with each other on daily basis, examples of these changes are shown below:

##### Positive:

- Easier connections: With the new, widely used social media platforms it has become much easier to find new people around you to socialize with. According to DataReportal.com, almost 6 (5.81) billion people in 2026 meet and connect via social media rather than searching for people in person. The research also shows that the rate of these virtual connections has grown by about 2.7 % since 2025 (Pew Research Center, 2024).
- Worldwide communication: The main point of social media is to allow people to connect or stay in touch regardless of the geographical distance between them. Social media allows two people that live in completely different sides of the world communicate with little to no delay.

##### Negative:

- Social isolation: Social media makes communication easier, but excessive use of it can cause loneliness and lead to social isolation. "Problematic social media use shows a moderate and consistent association with greater loneliness" (OECD, 2026).
- Mental health strain: As mentioned before, the development of social media has led to easier communications and connections, which also means it has led to easier spread of bullying. WHO states that around 1 in 6 teenagers have experienced cyberbullying (WHO, 2024).

#### 3.2.3. Politics

Although the social media in its largest is used for entertainment, it also serves as a useful tool in political side of many countries. The more developed and popular social media platforms in general become, the more they are used in political sphere.

##### Positive:

- Easier to share opinions: Many social media platforms have turned into fields of discussion of thoughts. Social media platforms where hundreds of people can meet and interact create a great opportunity for all sorts of discussions, especially on important topics such as politics (Pew Research Center, 2025).
- Promotion of viewpoints: Social media platforms make it easier to share  your opinions on political topics or the other way around, receive opinions of other people on a certain political topic.

##### Negative:

- More disinformation: It is an average thing for political opponents to post disinformation about each other, another problem is that people will very rarely double check the information they consume and hence anything they see will usually be accepted regardless of whether it is truth or not.
- Affected decision-making: Social media platforms can contribute to creation of so called echo chambers. An echo chamber is an environment in which one opinion or point of view is quickly spread and grown popular. People that find themselves within an echo chamber can have their original opinion either reinforced or completely changed, causing a real problem with forming a truly unaffected opinion. (PNAS, 2021).

## 4. Business Viewpoint Analysis
### 4.1. Defining the Business Viewpoint
The business viewpoint looks at money, competition, and value. So businesses always focus on making money, how to compete with other companies, and how they can grow their business. When we look at social media, we all have the same question: how are they making money, when we use it for free? But actually, the answer is they are making money from people's time and attention, mostly by showing adverts. So the more you see their ads, the more they make money.

### 4.2. MySpace
MySpace was one of the first big social media sites. In 2005, a company called News Corporation bought MySpace for $580 million (NPR, 2011).

#### Positive:
- It shows that social media is worth a lot of money, because a big company paid $580 million for it (NPR, 2011).
- At its peak, it had 70 million visitors per month (NPR, 2011).

#### Negative:
- When Facebook became more popular, a lot of people switched to it, and advertisers moved there too.
- MySpace was sold for only $35 million, which was a big loss for the company (NPR, 2011).

From a business perspective, this shows that having a lot of viewers alone is not enough; you always need to keep improving your app and follow trends, or people will move on.

### 4.3. Facebook
Facebook is free to use, but it makes almost all of its money from ads. When people use Facebook, they share things like their age, where they live, and what they like, so Facebook can show ads to the right people.

#### Positive:
- In 2024, Meta, the company that owns Facebook, made $164.5 billion, and $160.6 billion of that came from ads (Meta, 2025).
- By December 2024, around 3.35 billion people were using Meta's apps every day (Meta, 2025).

#### Negative:
- About 98% of its money comes from ads, so if advertisers stop paying, it could lose most of its income.
- Ad prices can go down too; in 2023, the average price per ad went down by 9% (Meta, 2025).

From a business viewpoint, this shows that information about users is very valuable, but relying on only one way to make money is risky.

### 4.4. Instagram
Instead of only competing with new apps, Facebook bought them. In April 2012, Facebook agreed to buy Instagram for about $1 billion (NPR, 2012). At that time, Instagram was a very small company with 13 employees (NPR, 2012), but it was growing very quickly and getting popular with young people.

#### Positive:
1. Facebook got rid of the competition that was growing. If Instagram had kept growing on its own, it could have taken most of the young users away from Facebook.
2. Later, Instagram became one of Meta's most important ways to make money, and it helped Meta compete really well with new apps like TikTok and others (Yahoo Finance, 2025).

#### Negative:
1. $1 billion was actually a lot of money for a company with only 13 employees (NPR, 2012). From a business viewpoint, it was a big risk for Facebook, and it might not have been worth it.
2. In 2018, the two people who made Instagram left Facebook because they did not agree with Mark Zuckerberg about how to run the app (MacRumors, 2018). This shows that when you buy a company, you can lose the people who made it successful.

From a business viewpoint, buying a competitor can be a smart way to stay on top, because you have less competition and people keep using your apps. But it costs a lot of money, and you can lose the people who made the app good.

### 4.5. TikTok
TikTok belongs to ByteDance, a company from China. It became popular very quickly because of its short videos and its "For You" page, which shows each person videos and things they would actually like. Like Facebook, it is free to use and makes most of its money from ads, but it also earns money from live streams and online shopping.

#### Positive:
1. TikTok's ad revenue is growing very fast. In 2025, it generated about $33.1 billion in ad revenue (Revenue Memo, 2025). This shows that advertisers want to be where young people are because they spend so much time on their phones.
2. It does not rely only on ads. People can buy gifts and send them to creators on live streams, and many creators make a lot of money from that. This brought in over $3 billion in 2025 (Revenue Memo, 2025).

#### Negative:
1. The US is TikTok's biggest market, so almost $12 billion in ad money was at risk when the US wanted to ban it (Search Engine Land, 2025).
2. In the end, ByteDance had to give control of TikTok in the US to a new company owned mostly by American investors, and ByteDance kept only 19.9% (TheJournal.ie, 2026).

From a business perspective, this shows that a new idea like short videos can beat older apps very quickly because people love them. But politics can be a big risk for a global business.
### 4.6. Connection to Module Concepts
This case study links to many key ideas from the module.

One major takeaway is how computing business models have changed. Social media apps are free to use, but they make a lot of money by selling users' attention to advertisers. This means the users are not really the customers. Advertisers are the customers because they are the ones who pay.

Along with attention, user data is one of the most valuable business assets today. Apps like Facebook and TikTok collect information about what people like, so they can show them the right ads, which leads to more people buying from those ads.

The case also highlights how computing infrastructure has evolved over time. Social media moved from websites on a computer, like MySpace, to mobile apps like Instagram and TikTok. These apps run on massive cloud infrastructure, enabling them to serve billions of users every day, underscoring how much technology has changed.

Lastly, it shows that companies must continually learn and adapt to new technology. MySpace did not keep up, so it lost its users to Facebook.

## 5. Ethical Reflections & Improvements
### 5.1. Identified Ethical Issues
#### 5.1.1. Attention as a Product
Because users do not pay to use social media, advertisers are the real customers, and platform revenue depends on keeping people scrolling for as long as possible. For instance, Meta earned $160.6 billion of its $164.5 billion total revenue in 2024 from advertising (Meta, 2025). This creates a conflict between making money and protecting user well-being. A small EEG study found that people with stronger signs of short-video addiction showed weaker cognitive control when dealing with distractions (Yan et al., 2024), while a meta-analysis linked internet-use-disorder symptoms to higher levels of loneliness (Mestre-Bach et al., 2025). Although these studies show a correlation rather than direct proof of harm, they highlight real risks for heavy users.

#### 5.1.2. Data and Privacy
Users share personal details like their age, location, and interests, which platforms use to target ads, a process that AI personalisation has made much more precise. TikTok's 2026 US restructuring, which moved American user data and the recommendation algorithm into a joint venture hosted on Oracle's US cloud servers, shows that data control has become a major political and security issue (Shepardson, 2026). Simply having users click "agree" at sign-up is a weak justification for tracking them continuously over time.

#### 5.1.3. Information and Harm
An analysis of over 100 million posts found that users on Facebook and Twitter mostly interact with sources they already agree with, which creates echo chambers (Cinelli et al., 2021). At the same time, a WHO/Europe report found that 15% of adolescents experienced cyberbullying in 2022, marking an increase for both boys and girls since 2018 (WHO, 2024).

#### 5.1.4. Competition and Accountability
When Facebook bought Instagram, it removed a fast-growing competitor, and Instagram's founders eventually left in 2018 following disagreements over how much independence the app should keep (Constine, 2018). Buying rivals helps protect a company's market share, but it also puts control over public communication into the hands of just a few firms. Furthermore, relying on advertising for roughly 98% of total revenue makes Meta's business model vulnerable if ad spending drops.

Still, the benefits of social media, like staying in touch across long distances, getting school updates outside term time, and joining political discussions, are real. The goal is not to get rid of social media, but to recognise that its downsides hit certain groups harder than others, especially young people. Research on corporate responsibility shows that customers, workers, regulators, and journalists all react differently when companies act irresponsibly (Cho et al., 2024). In the case of social media, users, parents, schools, advertisers, content creators, and regulators each face different trade-offs: young people carry most of the mental health risks, advertisers pay for attention, creators rely on algorithms for reach, and governments bear the cost of regulation. Any realistic solution needs to protect younger users without ruining the features that others find useful.

### 5.2. Proposed Improvements of Ethical Issues 
#### 5.2.1. Attention as a Product
##### Improvement:
- Non-personalised feed option.
- Default time limits.
- No infinite scroll for under-18s.
- No likes and comments features for under-18s.

##### Likely trade-off:
- Lower ad impressions.
- Lower user impressions.

#### 5.2.2. Data and Privacy
##### Improvement:
- Data storage minimisation.
- Opt-in option for sensitive targeting.
- Privacy preserving age checks.

##### Likely trade-off:
- Less precise targeting.

#### 5.2.3. Misinformation and Echo-Chambers
##### Improvement:
- AI-content labels.
- Independent audits of recommender algorithm systems.

##### Likely trade-off:
- Moderation cost.

#### 5.2.4. Dependence on Advertising
##### Improvement:
- Subscriptions.
- Shopping.
- Creator tools.

##### Likely trade-off:
- Slower growth at first.

#### 5.2.5. Accountability
##### Improvement:
- Board-level responsibility for well-being.
- Public transparency reports.

##### Likely trade-off:
- Reputational exposure.
- Disclosure exposure.

## 6. Conclusion
From MySpace to TikTok, every major social media platform has succeeded by finding better ways to capture user attention, often passing the negative side effects on to users and society until competitors or regulators stepped in.

### 6.1. Synthesis
While the main product changed in each era, from customisable profile pages and friend networks to photo feeds and short algorithmic videos, the core business model stayed the same: free access paid for by ads. Looking at the business side shows why this setup can be risky. MySpace dropped in value from $580 million to $35 million (CBS Boston, 2011), Meta relies on ads for about 98% of its income, and even though TikTok's 2025 ad revenue reached $33.1 billion (Revenue Memo, 2025), political pressure forced ByteDance to reduce its stake in its US operations to 19.9% (Shepardson, 2026). Looking at the societal side shows the trade-off: these platforms help people stay connected, share school updates, and discuss politics, but they also fuel distraction, loneliness, cyberbullying, and echo chambers. Both perspectives connect directly to the module's main concepts: users are the product rather than the paying customers, user data is a company's most valuable asset, and platforms that fail to adapt, like MySpace, quickly lose their audience.

### 6.2. Broader Implications
For social media companies, long-term competitive advantage is shifting from flashy new features to user trust, meaning that ethical design and early compliance with regulations are smart business moves. For governments, major platforms are increasingly being treated like essential public infrastructure, as seen in new age restrictions and TikTok's US data rules. As AI becomes even more common online, the same conflict between engagement and well-being will keep showing up unless companies design around it from the start. Finally, this case study is limited by its reliance on secondary sources, company reports, and correlational studies. Ultimately, the history of social media from MySpace to TikTok shows that the platforms built to last will be the ones that earn user attention responsibly.
